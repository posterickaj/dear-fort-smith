Fort Smith is waiting to feel the economic benefits of thosefancy fighter jets. Some people have estimated a $1 billion economic impact.Others are skeptical, saying we should have felt something by now.  

Where did the money go?

I asked my friends to pose a similar question to AI: San Franciscois more expensive than Fort Smith, so where does the extra money go?

I got 16 responses, and 16 said the extra money parks itself into land. 

That is why a family earns more in San Francisco but still feels poor.

Higher wages go towards higher rent paid to landlords.

Higher prices of daycare and haircuts and groceries go to paying their landlords,too.

This isn’t to say landowners are greedy or bad. In fact, the issue is preciselythere are not enough landowners. If the extra money only flows into land, we shouldwant as many landowners as possible. And since we can’t create more land, allwe can do is create more landowners. But how?

If we agree that economic growth is absorbed into land, and increased landvalues drives all other price increases, then we can think of those priceincreases as a land tax. Wherever there is economic growth, residents  pay “private land tax” to landowners: first throughpublic expenditures that attracts the economic growth, which fuels landappreciation, and second through increased rents, which act as ongoing recurringtaxes. Both streams of income incentivize land hoarding: the ROI for a vacantlot in downtown Fort Smith can be higher by letting it sit empty vs. building housingon it. The value of urban land is created by limiting the number of landownersand concentrating land tax into as few hands as possible. The fewer the landowners,the higher the premium that can be collected.

But we don’t have to pay private land tax to landowners. We could pay it toeach other. Under such a system, each landowner pays back the unearnedincrement of land value to those who created it: his neighbors. Americanfounding father Thomas Paine described such a taxation/repayment scenario for theUnited States, but we never pursued it. It’s not too late to try. And if local communitiesdesire to retain a portion of such a tax to fund police, fire, or utilities, therewould be no need for sales or property tax. We could take the land tax we’re alreadyspending and put it towards public good. If public investment creates landvalue, that value should come back to the public.
